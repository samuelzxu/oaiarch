Discussion and Context:
1. Geological and Environmental Setting  
   The study area on the interfluve of the Juruena–Teles Pires drainage is characterized by gently undulating upland plateaus (elevations ~300–350 m). Precipitation is seasonally high (>2,000 mm/yr) but concentrated in the wet season, leading to ephemeral surface flow on the plateaus. Native soils are well-drained oxisols; water control would have required substantial earthmoving.

2. Historical and Archaeological Significance  
   Pre‐Columbian populations in southern Amazonia are known to have built raised fields (“camellones”) and causeways primarily in seasonally flooded várzea and igapó. The detection of engineered ridges and channels on non‐floodplain uplands suggests a broader hydraulic strategy, challenging the assumption that earthworks were confined to wetlands. These anomalies align with regional findings of scattered upland embankments near the Xaraguara site (~100 km east).

3. Implications for Remote‐Sensing Archaeology  
   • Demonstrates the value of high‐resolution DTM derivatives (curvature and hillshade) to isolate low‐amplitude anthropogenic forms under closed canopy.  
   • Validates spectral cross‐checking (NIR reflectance <0.2) as an indicator of dark‐earth soils in forested areas.  
   • Underscores the need to mask modern linear infrastructure from OSM—but also to recognize that older forest‐management tracks often display sharper edges and regular spacing, unlike the irregular anomalies documented here.

4. Potential to Advance Theoretical Models  
   These findings support a more integrated model of Amazonian landscape engineering in which upland water storage and distribution networks complemented floodplain agriculture. They invite reconsideration of settlement and land‐use patterns away from major rivers, suggesting small, dispersed communities engineered micro‐catchments on interfluves.
