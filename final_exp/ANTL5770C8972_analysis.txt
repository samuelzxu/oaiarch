Discussion and Context  
The location near Bujari in the State of Acre sits in a classic upland interfluve environment of western Amazonia. Previous archaeological work has documented floodplain raised fields and earthworks in várzea contexts, but these upland structures extend the known distribution of pre‐Columbian water‐management systems into terra firme settings. The rectilinear canal (anomaly_1) would have intercepted seasonal overland flow, channeling excess rainwater into side ponds or distributary lanes. The adjacent raised platform (anomaly_2) likely served as an agricultural terrace or habitation mound, protected from saturated soils. The small circular hollows (anomaly_3) fit the pattern of platform‐house footings reported at other Acre interfluvial sites.

Broader Historical Implications  
This assemblage echoes engineering feats documented at interfluves in southwestern Amazonia (e.g., Acre knolls and Llanos de Moxos upland fields), but our detection in Bujari demonstrates that complex soil management and canopy‐cover cultivation were not confined to large riverbanks. It challenges floodplain‐centric models by showing that terra firme societies engineered microtopography and managed catchment hydrology on gentle slopes, suggesting more densely settled populations and greater ecological manipulation across the Amazon’s uplands than previously assumed.
