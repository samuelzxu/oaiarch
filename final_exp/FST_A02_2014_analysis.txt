Discussion and Context  
The region around 9RW8+HH São João, Oriximiná lies on an upland interfluve between tributaries of the Trombetas River. While classical Amazonian studies have focused on floodplain terra preta and large riverine earthworks, recent LiDAR surveys in western Pará have revealed sophisticated upland water‐management systems.  

Anomaly 1’s rectilinear bench fits the pattern of peripheral embankment-ditch pairs documented at sites like Monte Alegre and Tapajós, suggesting controlled bench-terrace construction. Anomaly 2 appears as a canal linking small drainage divides, aligning with known networks that captured and redistributed rainfall in otherwise well-drained upland soils. Anomaly 3’s parallel ridges strongly resemble raised-field agriculture documented at Kuhikugu and other interfluve locales, where prehistoric communities elevated and aerated soils for year-round cultivation.

These findings corroborate emerging theories that Amazonian uplands supported dense, engineered landscapes, challenging older models that confined complex hydraulic engineering to floodplain zones. By documenting linear canals and raised beds beneath closed canopy, we advance a more nuanced understanding of regional land-use complexity and pre-Columbian demographic dynamics.

Geographically, this sector of the Trombetas interfluve sits on nutrient-poor Oxisols; the detection of moisture-retentive canals and raised fields implies intentional soil enrichment (likely associated with dark-earth formation) and water retention strategies. Historically, this may represent a local variant of the Tapajós tradition, with radiocarbon dates elsewhere in Oriximiná pointing to intensive occupation between 1000 BP and 500 BP.
