Contextual Interpretation and Historical Significance  
The Barreirinha interfluve (9M4F+29) lies within a nexus of documented terra preta and geoglyph complexes dated to AD 500–1200. Historically, this sector has seen intensive management of seasonally‐flooded terraces and upland soils via raised fields and canal networks. The semicircular bund system (anomaly 1) parallels structures excavated at nearby Oriximiná sites, but its scale and curvature suggest a previously unrecorded variation on water‐retention basins. Likewise, the long causeway (anomaly 2) may have facilitated year‐round transport across inundated plains, aligning with ethnohistorical reports of canoe‐road hybrids. The rectangular terrace (anomaly 3) expands known footprint of formal plazas or platform villages in this part of the Lower Amazon. Finally, anomaly 4’s regular array hints at modular raised‐field agriculture, complementing theories of small‐scale household plots.

These findings refine the model of Amazonian hydraulic landscapes by showing:
- Greater morphological diversity in embankment geometry (semicircles, rectangles, regular arrays).
- Integration of transport features (causeways) with agricultural cells in a continuous engineered network.
- Evidence for localized, high‐resolution planning extending across elevations formerly considered marginal for intensive cultivation.

By documenting both large‐scale infrastructural axes and compact raised‐field clusters, this survey challenges the binary categorization of Amazonian pre‐Columbian landscapes as solely ‘tectonic geoglyph’ vs. ‘extensive raised‐field’ zones. Instead, it supports a continuum of water‐management strategies adapted to micro‐topographic variability and seasonal hydrology.
