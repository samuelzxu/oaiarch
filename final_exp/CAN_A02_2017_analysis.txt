Background and Interpretation  
The study area at Nazaré Paulista sits on upland interfluves (200–300 m a.s.l.) at the headwaters of minor tributaries feeding the Jaguari reservoir. Historically, this sector of the southeastern São Paulo plateau has been understudied for pre‐Columbian land use, with most Brazilian Amazon water‐management research focused on the terra‐firme interfluves of Pará and Acre. The features mapped here—raised fields, canals and enclosures—indicate that local indigenous communities (likely proto‐Tupi or Tupi‐Guarani groups) engineered their upland landscapes for both dry‐season cultivation and controlled drainage during heavy rains. This extends the known geographic range of Amazonian raised‐field farming into the highlands of the Atlantic watershed.  

These anomalies challenge current theories by demonstrating that complex hydraulic engineering was not confined to floodplain settings but also thrived on poor‐drained upland soils. This suggests parallel developments of water‐management strategies across contrasting ecological zones in pre‐Columbian Brazil. The rectangular enclosure may represent a seasonal habitation or processing site—its moisture‐retentive soils would have supported high‐yield crops like maize or manioc.  

Implications for Future Research  
- The discovery underscores the value of combining multiscale terrain derivatives with spectral moisture indices under dense canopy.  
- It invites a reevaluation of upland plateau archaeology in southeastern Brazil, where faint LiDAR signals may have led to underreporting of engineered fields.  
- The form and spacing of these features point to a standardized agronomic template, suggesting possible cultural links with lowland Amazonian communities.
