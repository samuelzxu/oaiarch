Discussion:

1. Regional Context  
This sector of the Acre interfluve, between the Madeira and Purus Rivers, lies within the core area of Late Holocene terra preta construction and raised‐field agriculture. Pre‐Columbian groups in the Santa Maria–Senador Guiomard area are known from patchy survey data but have not previously been documented building elaborate upland water‐management systems.

2. Historical Significance  
The discovery of rectilinear raised fields and drainage canals in a high‐interfluve setting extends the known range of Amazonian hydraulic engineering beyond floodplain and várzea contexts. It suggests these societies adapted water‐management to seasonally drought‐prone uplands, using raised beds to prevent root waterlogging during heavy rains and to retain moisture in the dry season.

3. Theoretical Implications  
Current models posit that intensive earthworks in Amazonia concentrate on floodplains or seasonally inundated llanos. These anomalies demonstrate that engineered agrarian landscapes also existed in non‐alluvial uplands—challenging the notion of a floodplain-centric distribution of monumental hydraulic features. It implies a broader variability in pre‐Columbian land‐use strategies and soil‐management regimes.

4. Integration with Modern Data  
Cross‐check with OSM confirms that none of these features coincide with mapped roads or recent clearings. The spectral anomalies in the NIR band (moisture retention signatures) strongly correlate with the DTM-identified features, reinforcing their anthropogenic origin rather than modern logging or trails.

5. Preservation and Next Steps  
Field validation via targeted coring for terra preta signatures and test pitting along the edges of the raised platforms will confirm construction methods, carbon content, and dating. High‐resolution drone imagery under varying seasonal moisture conditions could refine the planar extents of the ditch networks.
